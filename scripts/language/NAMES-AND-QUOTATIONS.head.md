# Names and quotations in sixteen languages

This file is generated by `scripts/language/build_card_text.py`. Do not edit it by hand: change the
sources and run the script again. It records, for every card, where its name and its quotation in each
language came from, and it lists everything that was translated for the game rather than taken from a
source, and everything that is missing.

English is the source of truth (`src/lib/cards.ts`) and is not repeated in the per-language tables.

## What the words in the "source" columns mean

**Names**

- `wikidata`: the label of the Wikidata item in that language, fetched from the Wikidata API
  (`wbgetentities`). Licence CC0 1.0. A disambiguating bracket at the end of a label ("Portia (The
  Merchant of Venice)") is cut off. Chinese uses the `zh-hans` label, then `zh-cn`, then `zh`. For the
  languages written in Latin letters (es, fr, de, it, pt, pl, vi, id) the item's `mul` label is used when
  the language has no label of its own: Wikidata defines `mul` as the label for every language that has
  none. It is not used for other scripts, where a Latin-letter name would not be a name in that language.
  Portuguese takes the item's `pt-br` label first, where Wikidata has one, because the game's Portuguese is
  Brazilian (the interface, `src/i18n/locales/pt.ts`): "Marco Antônio", not "Marco António", and "Lord
  Byron". Those labels were fetched separately (`docs/language/sources/wikidata-pt-br.json`), so no other
  language's labels moved with them.
- `wikipedia`: Wikidata has no label, but that language's Wikipedia has an article on the item; its title is used.
  One name is spelled as that language's Wikipedia writes it instead of as Wikidata labels it: Portuguese
  "Próspero" (TP-001), because Wikidata's `pt` and `pt-br` label "Prospero" is the verb *prospero*, "I
  prosper" (LanguageTool's LP_PARONYMS rule flagged it), and pt.wikipedia.org's article on the play, "A
  Tempestade" (revision 72539005, `docs/language/sources/ptwiki-a-tempestade.json`), writes Próspero 17 times
  and Prospero once. The relic name and the scenes follow the card. Three more take the Wikipedia title
  where the label is not the character's usual name in that language: Russian «Горацио» (the label
  «Гораций» is the Roman poet Horace; ru.wikipedia titles the character «Горацио (персонаж)»), Polish
  «Banko» (the label is the English spelling) and Italian «Ulisse» (it.wikipedia's title; the label is
  «Odisseo»). Each reason is in `TITLE_FIRST` in the build script.
- `published`: Wikidata's label is wrong or is the English one, and the published translation this record
  quotes for the play prints the character's name: French «La Nourrice» (Hugo's speaker label; Wikidata's
  «Infirmière» is a hospital nurse, and she is Juliet's wet-nurse) and «Frère Laurence» (Hugo; no French
  label), German «Bruder Lorenzo» (Schlegel's stage directions; Wikidata's German label was the English
  «Friar Laurence»). The page, its revision and the count of the printed form are in
  `docs/language/sources/published-names.json`, and a test holds each name to that record.
- Two labels are replaced by one of the item's own aliases, with the reason recorded: Chinese «奶妈» for the
  Nurse (the label 護士 is a hospital nurse; the alias is 奶媽), and Spanish «Conde Paris» (the label's
  «París» is the city, stressed on its last syllable; Menéndez y Pelayo prints the character «Páris», stressed
  on the first, which today's spelling writes Paris).
- `derived`: cut by a fixed rule from a sourced label. Three rules exist. (1) The card shows one word of a
  longer name ("Brutus" from "Marcus Junius Brutus", "Cassius", "Bottom"): the word in the same position is
  taken when the label in that language has the same number of words, otherwise the whole label is used.
  (2) A title character whose own item has no label in the language takes the name from the title of the
  play in that language ("Hamlet", "Macbeth"; "Romeo" and "Juliet" from the two halves of the title).
  (3) Byron's Vietnamese and Thai labels add his peerage ("Nam tước Byron thứ 6", "บารอนไบรอนที่ 6", "6th
  Baron Byron"); the card takes the personal name before it, as the other languages' labels give it.
- `translated`: the game's own invention (relics, potions, "Lord Montague", "Lady Capulet", houses,
  elements). Written for the game by the language model that did this work. **Not reviewed by a native
  speaker.** Checked by tools that did not write it (see "Checks by tools that did not write the text");
  a name with no finding is not thereby right.
- `kept`: SM-001, "The Sonnet Man", the product name, the same in every language: neither sourced nor translated.
- `missing`: no source has the name in that language. Nothing was invented: the card shows the English
  name there, and the invocation accepts the English name.

**Aliases** (other names a character is called by, for the invocation) are Wikidata's aliases in the
language, except those the build leaves out by rule (`alias_rejected`): one with anything but letters in it
(a description: 科學怪人之母：瑪麗雪萊, "mother of Frankenstein: Mary Shelley"; 济慈，J.), one in two scripts (Italian
«Aφροδίτη», a Latin A and Greek letters) or in Latin letters in a language written otherwise (the English name
counts anyway) or wholly in another script than the language's own (Greek «Ἀΐδης» in Russian), the name of the
Roman god Wikidata says is this Greek one (P460, a Roman deity) where English names the two differently (Vietnamese
«Jupiter» and «Venus», Russian «Плутон» and «Меркурий», Polish «Pluton», Korean 플루톤, Spanish «Pluto»; not Apollo),
and, in Chinese, Japanese and Thai, the name or another alias with more added (ゼウス神像, "statue of Zeus";
ブルトゥス家, "the Brutus family"). Others were left out by hand, each with its reason in `NOT_ALIAS`: German
«Abadonna» and «Uthuce», Chinese «哈里斯», Russian «Дит» (Dis Pater) and «Орк» (Orcus, whom Wikidata does not link to
Hades; «орк» is also an orc), and the misspellings. Every alias left out is listed under "Wikidata aliases not taken
as names".

**Quotations**

- `published`: copied by program from a published translation that is in the public domain. The text is the
  span matched in the transcription at the URL given (a permanent link to the revision read). Line breaks
  of verse are shown as " / ", footnote marks and the page-number marks of the transcription ("[p. 160
  modifica]") are removed, and a leading or trailing dash is cut; nothing else is changed. François-Victor
  Hugo's long dashes inside a line mark the verse lines of the original and are his. In the Gutenberg texts,
  where prose is wrapped at a fixed width, a line end inside prose is a space, not a verse break, and
  Gutenberg's "--" (its plain-text sign for a dash) is shown as "—". Spelling is the
  edition's: Spanish "Porqué", "dejaria", Portuguese "Horacio", "cahindo", Japanese old character forms
  and historical kana (Tsubouchi, 1933) are as printed. For a Japanese line in old character forms the
  module also keeps its modern reading (`echo`), made character for character by OpenCC's JPVariants
  table (the table of OpenCC's `t2jp` conversion), so that a player typing 歓楽 echoes Tsubouchi's 歡樂;
  the printed forms count too, and the card shows the line as printed. Kana spelling is not modernised.
- `NEW, unsourced`: no public-domain translation of the line was found in that language (or none was
  searched for; see "What was not done"). The line was translated for the game by the language model that
  did this work. **It is generated text, not reviewed by a native speaker.** It was put through the tool
  checks below (spelling, grammar where LanguageTool has the language, back-translation), which can catch
  some errors and cannot show a line to be right. Do not present it as a published translation.

The quotations on the mythology cards (OL-, UW-) are not quotations from any work: they are lines written
for the game in English. They can only ever be translated, never sourced.

## How the game uses this

- `src/lib/cards-i18n/<lang>.ts` holds the names, lines, scenes, labels and stop words of one language. It is
  loaded with the interface language (for display) and with a duel's language pack (`loadPack`).
- Signature: in a duel fought in a language that has its own line for the card, the telling words are the
  words of that line that are long enough for the language (`lang.ts`) and are not function words: not on
  stopwords-iso's list, not filed by Wiktionary as a pronoun, particle, conjunction, preposition,
  postposition, determiner or article (a headword is left out of that only when every sense of it in the
  class is a variant spelling, a heading with no definition, a misspelling, slang, or the usage of one region:
  Vietnamese "hè", a Central particle, is also "summer"), not a Korean such word with its particle (나는, "I"),
  not a Chinese or Japanese word made only of stop characters (你的, "your"), and in Japanese not a word with no
  kanji or katakana (particles, auxiliaries and endings are written in hiragana: した, あなた). French and
  Italian elided articles are taken off first (c’est is "est", l’amour is "amour"). The card shows a telling
  word as the line writes it (German "Rose", not "rose").
- A word of the verse echoes a telling word when it is the same word as the language counts it: the same
  Snowball stem in Spanish, French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Polish, Russian, Arabic, Hindi and
  Indonesian (Russian «Гамлета» and «Гамлет», Spanish «rosas» and «rosa»); in Vietnamese the same syllable
  with the same tone (ngủ "sleep" is not ngu, nor the start of người; hoà and hòa are one spelling), and a word of
  the line of more than one syllable that Wiktionary lists as a noun, verb or adjective is one telling word, said
  when its syllables come in a row («vấn an», "to ask after", does not say «vấn đề», "problem"); in Korean
  the same word with or without its particles (햄릿이 says 햄릿); in Chinese, Japanese and Thai, whole words of
  the verse (`Intl.Segmenter`) that spell it in a row (Japanese: whose letters begin with it, since a verb's
  ending is often cut off as a word of its own). Thai SARA AM is compared in one piece (stopwords-iso writes
  ทํา and สําหรับ in two). A verse word that is itself a stop word never echoes.
- How many: as the engine asks of the English line (`signatureNeeded`), a line with three or more telling words
  needs two of them, a line with one or two needs one. They are counted in the spelling players type (歓楽 and
  the printed 歡樂 are one word), and one verse word counts once even when it says a word of the line and the
  same English word (German «Rose» and "rose"). The hint lists the words with the language's comma (、 in
  Chinese and Japanese, the Arabic comma in Arabic).
- Korean and Thai lines are also read by analysers made for those languages (`scripts/language/check/segment.py`,
  on a build host): Kiwi gives each Korean word's first morpheme and part of speech, and a word whose first
  morpheme is a pronoun, an auxiliary (않은, 볼까요, 주세요), the copula, a determiner or a conjunctive adverb, a
  bound noun not after a number (것이지요; but 한 바퀴, "one round", is a unit and counts), or a verb on the stop list
  (있어요, of 있다) goes on the stop list; an interjection does not (만세 is "hail"). PyThaiNLP's newmm reads a Thai
  line with its dictionary and the card names, and where ICU cuts one of its words into pieces the signature
  takes the pieces together (โรสแมรี, "rosemary", which ICU reads as โรส, "rose", and แมรี, "Mary"), unless a piece
  is a function word (ขอให้, "let", stays ขอ and ให้). The third check had found Ophelia's line landing on two
  such fragments in both languages.
- In Japanese a run of katakana is one word: ICU cuts a name or loanword it does not know into pieces
  (ヨリック into ヨ and リック, ホレイショー into ホレイ and ショー), and MeCab, the analyser the IPA dictionary was made
  for, groups such a run into one word (its `char.def` gives KATAKANA the GROUP flag), as this does.
- Reciting the line is refused: four of its words running, as in English, or the whole of a line of two or
  three words. In Chinese, Japanese and Thai the segmenter's words are often single particles, so a recital
  there is a run of the line's letters as long as four English words of the game's lines are in that
  language: Chinese 6, Japanese 9, Thai 19 (letters with their marks), measured as four times the median
  letters per English word over the 71 lines, and measured again by a test. The English line's words still
  count (except a word of the duel's language spelled like one: Italian "dove" is a stop word there), and
  reciting the English line is still refused.
- Invocation: every character's full name in the duel's language counts, and each one-word alias. Of a longer
  name, where the English card has one name (Romeo, Hamlet) each word of the local label counts alone (Roméo
  Montaigu: Roméo, Montaigu); where the English card has a longer one (Mary Shelley, Mark Antony, Lady Macbeth),
  only its last word, as the engine counts English names ("mary" and "mark" name no one), and in Korean, which
  puts a title after the name, the last word that is not a title (맥베스 부인: 맥베스). A single word does not
  count when it is a stop word or an ordinary word of the language by Hunspell
  (`run.sh names`; the rule is `common_name_words` in the build script): a title such as Russian «граф»,
  Vietnamese «Phu nhân» or «Lãnh chúa», or Russian «Кора» (bark), an alias of Persephone. Where the script has
  letter case Hunspell is asked in lower case, so a name the dictionary holds is not taken for a word; in
  German (whose nouns are capitalised) and in Arabic, Hindi and Thai (which have no case) a word Hunspell knows
  is taken for a title only before the last word of the name, where those languages put titles; Korean puts
  the title after the name (맥베스 부인), so there any word Hunspell knows is. A word that is also a word of an
  English character name (Romeo, Lady, William) counts, as it does in English. Where names decline (Russian,
  Polish, German, Arabic), a name counts as itself or as one of the forms the card text lists for it (`forms`):
  `scripts/language/check/declension.py` makes them by one paradigm per ending, singular only, written out by
  hand (Russian «Джульетта»: Джульетты, Джульетте, Джульетту, Джульеттой; Polish «Hamlet»: Hamleta, Hamletowi,
  Hamletem, Hamlecie; German the genitive -s; Arabic the conjunctions و and ف and the prepositions ب, ك and ل
  before the name, as Snowball's Arabic stemmer strips them), and a Russian name in -о, -е, -и or -у, or a Polish
  one in -e, has none («Банко», «Ромео», «Kore»). Then `run.sh forms` puts each to Hunspell (`hstem.cpp`, its
  `stem()`), and a form the dictionary knows as an ordinary word is dropped: in Russian and Polish one it accepts
  in lower case («Марка», "of a stamp"; Polish «Banku», "of a bank"), in German and Arabic one it takes for a form
  of another word (Arabic «كبو», "stumbling", made of «بو» for Poe). A form that is also another proper noun is kept
  and listed (Russian «Афины», Athens and Athena's genitive; «Клавдия», also the name Klavdiya; Polish «Ateny»),
  as English keeps "Paris". Every form, kept or dropped, with Hunspell's entries for it, is in
  `docs/language/checks/name-forms.json`. A declined form keeps the name's capital where the name has one
  («Персефону», «Julię»; not «медузу», a jellyfish). Before this, a name took any case ending after its stem,
  and the third check (2026-11-17) found common words invoking: Russian «Дитя» ("child", from the alias «Дит»),
  «Банки» ("banks"), «Афинах» ("in Athens"), Polish «Banku», «Bańka» ("bubble"), «Kora» ("bark"), Arabic «بانكوك»
  (Bangkok), «هادسون» (Hudson), «الأخيلة» ("imaginations"). `accidents.test.ts` now says each of the words of
  the 50,000 commonest in film subtitles that come near a name (Hermit Dave's FrequencyWords, from OpenSubtitles
  2018, in eleven languages: `scripts/language/fetch/near_names.py`, `docs/language/sources/opensubtitles-near-names.json`),
  as written and with a capital, and only names and their listed forms invoke; the forms that occur there were
  read one by one and are in the test. Names match with particles in Korean, as whole words in
  Chinese, Japanese and Thai, and exactly elsewhere («prosperidad» is not «Próspero», «marche» is not «Marc»),
  where a verse word with an accent the name does not have is another word (typed without accents, a name
  still counts; Spanish «París» is the city, Count Paris is «Paris»). "The Sonnet Man" counts only whole.
- A name that is also an ordinary word of its language counts as English counts "Bottom" and "Nurse" (round 3,
  second check, which found Spanish and Italian «Bruto», Italian «Banco», German «Zettel», Japanese パック and
  ブレイク, Hindi बैंको and Thai โพ invoking by themselves). The card text lists them (`ordinary`): English
  Wiktionary's part of speech for the word (`fetch/name_pos.py`: a Noun, Verb, Adjective or the like with a
  definition of its own or a form of another word, not only the character's English name, as German «Brutus» is
  filed, nor only another spelling), and where the script has capitals Hunspell's acceptance of the word written
  small as well: the two must agree, because Polish Hunspell alone accepts «hamlet» and «zeus» and Wiktionary alone
  has rare words such as Italian «achille». Where a language writes its ordinary words small (es fr it pt pl ru vi
  id), such a name counts by itself only written with a capital inside a sentence («y Bruto alzó el puñal», not
  «Bruto es el sueldo», «¡Bruto!» or a line that opens on it); German writes its nouns with a capital, and Arabic,
  Hindi, Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Thai have no capitals, so there it never counts by itself, only inside a
  longer name (ウィリアム・ブレイク, メアリー・シェリー). Bottom, whom English calls on only as "Nick Bottom", "Bully
  Bottom" or "Bottom the weaver", counts in no language by one word (French «Bottom», German «Zettel», 波顿);
  English's phrases still count everywhere. The Nurse, whom English counts as "the nurse" and never "a nurse" or
  "nurse", counts by her whole local name: in German only after a definite article («die Wärterin», «der Wärterin»;
  not «eine Wärterin»), in Russian only with a capital inside a sentence («и Кормилица»; not «наша кормилица», "our
  breadwinner"), in Chinese as written (奶妈), in French as «La Nourrice», and never by a word of a longer name
  (French «Nourrice», Spanish «Nodriza»). In another language's duel an English name that is an ordinary word there
  counts as that language's own name does: Italian, Spanish and Portuguese «medusa» (a jellyfish), Italian «rossetti»
  and French «viola» only with a capital inside a sentence, German "Puck" (the ice-hockey puck) not by itself. Italian
  «viola» ("purple") still counts for Viola through her English name, because Italian has no name for her here and so
  no verdict on the word. The cost is plain: Puck in Japanese, Banquo in Hindi and Achilles in
  Arabic (أخيل is also "I imagine") are now invoked only by their English names there (Puck in German by «Puk», «Droll»
  or "Puck"), and a Spanish,
  Italian, Portuguese, Polish or Russian line that opens on «Bruto», «Banco», «Próspero», «Medusa», «Пак» or
  «Одиссей» invokes no one.
- Chinese, Japanese and Thai are read by ICU's segmenter, which cuts a word it does not know into pieces, and a
  piece could be a name (安东尼奥, "Antonio", into 安东尼 and 奥; ららぽーと, a mall, into pieces one of which, as
  katakana, was ポー). A name in katakana now counts only as a whole run of katakana written in katakana.
  A Chinese name is not one inside a longer foreign name: Chinese spells a foreign name in the characters of Xinhua's
  transliteration tables, and ICU cuts one it does not know into single characters (哈德斯菲尔德, Huddersfield, into
  哈, 德, 斯, 菲, 尔, 德, with 哈德斯, Hades, in it). A name is a piece of a longer one when the word beside it is such a
  character that is no word by itself (菲, 迪, 娅), a longer word of them that is not a common word (海德, 罗斯), or a
  character that is a word (多, 特) with more pieces beyond it (塔克西多帕克, Tuxedo Park); a common word (当然, 东西),
  a function word or any other character ends the foreign name. A function character the segmenter joined to the name
  (修了 in 赫瑞修了) is not part of it. Where the piece beside the name is one such character that is also a word by itself
  (雷, 森, 格, 托, 特, 松, 登, 希, 庫), the name is a piece when a middle dot meets the far edge of the pieces or the name's
  other edge (保罗·格罗塞蒂, 托罗密欧·司卡皮, 朱麗葉特·劉易斯), when pieces stand on both sides (里奧布朗庫子爵), when one such
  character after the name ends the clause or comes before 是 (布莱克雷, 王子班柯希, 布莱克森是演员), or when one opens the
  clause before the name and 是 follows it (梯丟斯是英雄): the engine's fourth check (2026-11-18) found these on
  zh.wikipedia, and `echo.ts foreignAround` refuses 14 of the 19 it listed; the same Tatoeba recall run below refused
  the same 38 sentences with and without it. Still read as a name: one such character before a name with nothing to
  mark it (拉帕里斯伯爵, 托帕克大桥, 阿不都拉哈德斯), and a common word of those characters beside it (哈德斯克拉布尔: 克拉,
  "carat"; 普羅丟斯: 普罗); 拉 in 拉朱丽叶的手 ("pulls Juliet's hand") is that word. A Thai name counts only where the letters beside it are words the invocation
  knows, two in a row where there are that many (the pronunciation table's 17,219 Wiktionary words, loaded with the
  duel's sound data, and the card text's own; one letter is never a word, though the table lists the letters), and a
  two-letter word before it only after another word: เพอร์ in เพอร์เซอุส (Perseus) and ซาน in ซานแอนโทนีโอ (San Antonio)

  The engine's fourth check (2026-11-18) found the limit of this rule, still open: a foreign name whose syllables beside
  the name happen to be Thai words still invokes Zeus (แม่น้ำออกซุส, the Oxus: ออก, "out"; ยูโดซุส, Eudoxus; ฟรีซุส,

## What was not done, and what has not been checked

Read this before treating any language as finished.

- **Published lines** were taken where a public-domain translation is transcribed on Wikisource or Project
  Gutenberg and the line could be found in it: Italian 51 of 71 cards (Rusconi's complete prose Shakespeare
  of 1858–1859, all eleven plays, and Ragazzoni's Raven), French 51 (Hugo; Mallarmé's Raven, Saladin's
  Frankenstein, Rabbe's Shelley), German 50 (Schlegel's Romeo, Dream, Merchant and Julius Caesar; Dorothea
  Tieck's Macbeth; Wieland's 1762–1766 prose for Hamlet, the Tempest, Twelfth Night, Othello, Lear and Henry
  IV; Lachmann's Raven, Strodtmann's Shelley), Russian 34 (Mikhalovsky's Romeo, Gnedich's Hamlet, Satin's Tempest,
  Kozlov's Julius Caesar, Weinberg's Othello and Merchant, Kroneberg's Twelfth Night, and the poems), Spanish 29,
  Polish 28, Japanese 13 (Romeo and Juliet only), Portuguese 6. Arabic, Hindi, Chinese, Korean, Vietnamese, Thai and Indonesian have none. Every
  other line is `NEW, unsourced`.
- **Where a published translation exists but was not used**, and why (also listed under "Published lines found
  and not used" below): Russian LD-002, Gumilyov's Rime of 1919, set aside in the third pass while its Russian
  copyright is in doubt. He was executed in 1921 and rehabilitated in 1992; the Civil Code counts a rehabilitated
  author's term from the year after rehabilitation (art. 1281 p. 5), and whether that reaches a work whose term had
  already run out is a matter of how art. 6 of the law that put part IV into force is read. Two readings in this
  project disagreed and neither is a lawyer's. ru.wikisource carries the text as public domain. Apollon
  Korinfsky's version of 1894 (ru.wikisource «Старый моряк (Кольридж)») was looked at as a replacement: it is too
  free to give the line ("Water, water, every where"). The card has the game's own line, marked new, until a
  lawyer settles it. Earlier rounds:
  - German: Schlegel–Tieck's Hamlet, Tempest, Twelfth Night, Othello and Lear are in the public domain, but
    Project Gutenberg's German files for those plays (its index GUTINDEX.ALL, read from the official mirror
    gutenberg.pglaf.org because www.gutenberg.org answered HTTP 503 all day on 2026-11-16) are Wieland's
    1762–1766 prose, the first German Shakespeare; that is what was used. zeno.org, which has the
    Schlegel–Tieck texts, could not be read by the fetch script. Wieland's spelling is his (seyn,
    zurük, Crone). Neither Wieland nor this Gutenberg text has the Tempest's epilogue or "I'll drown my book"
    in a form that was found, so those two German lines are NEW. The Gutenberg Macbeth reads "entweiht mich"
    where Tieck printed "entweibt mich" (unsex me); the German line for Lady Macbeth stops before that word.
  - Polish and Russian: the plays are cached and searched; a line was taken only when it could be found and
    recognised with certainty (by its speaker and the lines around it). Twenty Russian lines were added in this
    round from the same transcriptions (Romeo, Hamlet, the Tempest, Twelfth Night, the Merchant). Not found in
    the time spent: Cordelia's "it is thy business that I go about" in Druzhinin's Lear, Olivia's "Love sought
    is good" in Kroneberg, and the Polish lines still marked NEW.
    The Polish Macbeth page (Ulrich) could not be fetched under the title tried. The cached Russian Dream
    (Grigoryev) and Macbeth (Yuryev) are transcriptions in the pre-1918 spelling; lines were not taken from
    them, because players would not type that spelling, and modern-spelling versions were not looked for.
  - Spanish: Menéndez y Pelayo's 1881 versions omit some lines (the Nurse's "drudge", Lady Capulet's
    "marry"); Spanish Wikisource has no transcribed Tempest, Twelfth Night, Dream, Lear or Henry IV part 2 in a
    public-domain translation that was found. Astrana Marín's Julio César is on Wikisource but he died in
    1959 (Spain: life plus 80), so it was not used.
  - Portuguese: Rebelo da Silva's Otelo (1856) and Luís I's O Mercador de Veneza (1879) are listed on
    pt.wikisource, but the page titles tried did not exist; not followed further.
  - Japanese: only Tsubouchi's Romeo and Juliet is on ja.wikisource, and it is the only one of his Shakespeare
    translations that Aozora Bunko has published. Aozora's page for him (index_pages/person141.html, read
    2026-11-16) lists his Hamlet, Macbeth, Othello, King Lear, Julius Caesar, the Tempest (颶風), the Dream,
    the Merchant and the Sonnets under 作業中の作品, works being prepared: they have no card page and no
    text yet (card1491.html answers 404). When they are published, `scripts/language/fetch/aozora.py` reads
    them; it was tested on the published Romeo and Juliet (card 42773), where it reproduces all 13 lines
    taken from Wikisource.
  - Chinese: zh.wikisource has a 1924 classical-Chinese Hamlet (漢姆萊脫) whose translator's dates were not
    established, and a contributors' own translation of Sonnet 18 (CC BY-SA); neither was used. Korean
    Wikisource has a Hamlet page whose translator was not established. Arabic Wikisource has The Merchant of
    Venice and Sonnet 18; their translators were not established.
  - The Romantic poems: Polish (Beaupré, 1910) has a Raven whose translator's dates Wikidata does not have,
    so it was not used; the Russian "She Walks in Beauty" on Wikisource is a 2002 translation, not used.
    Wordsworth, Keats, Blake, Byron, Browning and Rossetti were not searched for in any language.
  - No library other than Wikisource was searched.
- **The place of each published line** was recognised by reading the passage around the match (speaker,
  neighbouring lines), not by act, scene and line numbers in the translation. The citation given is that of
  the English line. Some 19th-century prose translations render a line loosely (Rusconi's "Ti troverò al
  chiaro di luna, superba Titania" for "Ill met by moonlight"); the published wording is used as it stands.
- **Portuguese is Brazilian.** The interface is Brazilian Portuguese; the card text first written for the game
  was European (pirilampos, sumo, "a brilhar", "Marco António"). In this round every scene, every line
  written for the game and the potion name "Sumo da flor de Puck" were rewritten in Brazilian usage, and a
  scene names a character as its card does ("Mercutio", "Benvolio", "Tybalt", "Odisseu"). The rewrite is
  again one language model's output, put through the same checks. The published lines keep their editions'
  text: Luís I's Hamlet (Lisbon, 1877) is European Portuguese of its time; Machado de Assis is Brazilian.
- **Everything translated for the game is one language model's output**: every `translated` name and label,
  every `NEW` line and every scene in every language. The scenes in Arabic, Hindi, Vietnamese and Thai were
  written in this round. In those scenes, a character whose name has no source in the language keeps the
  English name in Latin letters, as the card does, rather than an invented transliteration. None of this text
  has been reviewed by a native speaker. It was checked by tools that did not write it (the section "Checks by
  tools that did not write the text" below says what they found and what they cannot find); a text with no
  finding is not thereby correct.
- **Wikidata labels are community-edited and a few look doubtful.** They are used as found. For review:
  Russian Horatio is labelled "Гораций" (the usual stage name is Горацио); Chinese Macbeth is "马克白", the
  Taiwan form, converted to Simplified characters (the mainland form is 麦克白); Romans carry their Latin
  names (Japanese ブルトゥス for Brutus, where the stage tradition says ブルータス); some poets carry a full
  formal name (Vietnamese and Thai Byron). Translated relic names follow these labels.
- **The stop-word lists are broad.** stopwords-iso counts some content words as stop words. Two were taken
  off it because they are the one telling word of Hamlet's line: Portuguese "questão" and Russian "вопрос"
  (`NOT_STOP` in the build script). Wiktionary's function-word classes contain homographs, which are left out
  of the telling words with them: Thai เวลา (time; also "when"), หน้า (face; "in front of"), เพื่อน (friend; an
  old pronoun), Vietnamese thần (god; an old "I"), trái (fruit, left; "contrary to"), thành (city; "into"),
  German "fern", Polish "dobra". Otherwise the lists are used as published. A line with no telling word of its
  own leaves the English words to decide, as before. Seven lines have none although the English line has some
  telling words: hi UW-002, zh LD-004, ja VC-003, ja PT-005, ja OL-004, vi SM-001 and th LD-004 (their words are
  function words, or shorter than the language's measure, or single Chinese or Japanese characters, which do not
  count: 花, 草, 愛, 力); and Viola's IL-001 ("I am not what I am") in es, fr, de, pt, ja, vi and id, whose English
  line, all little words, the engine now echoes by its phrase "not what". A test pins that list.
- **Accidental landings** were measured over every pack's written-opponent and acrostic verses, which were
  not written for any card, against every character card (`accidents.test.ts`). In the third pass (Mac Studio,
  2026-11-17), with the engine's two-word rule: English lands the signature on 0.18% of verse–card pairs and the
  invocation on none; every other language is at or under 0.33% for the signature (Arabic; Vietnamese 0.27%)
  and none for the invocation. The second pass measured English at 2.2% and 3.8% under the old one-word rule,
  with Vietnamese at 6.0% and a German 2.3% that the check showed to be «wartest» invoking the Nurse through a
  shared stem, not the Nurse's name. Before the second pass: Vietnamese 12.5% and 31%, Thai 10.4%, Korean
  4.7%, Japanese 3.9%. These are the packs' own 34 to 53 verses per language; no player verses were measured.
- **German Hamlet is Wieland's.** "Seyn oder nicht seyn—Das ist die Frage" is Wieland's 1766 prose (Gutenberg
  #7276), where the file has "Seyn oder nicht seyn--Das ist die Frage"; "--" is Gutenberg's plain-text dash and
  is shown as "—". Schlegel's canonical "Sein oder Nichtsein, das ist hier die Frage" was looked for again
  in this round and not found: not in Project Gutenberg's index (GUTINDEX.ALL from gutenberg.pglaf.org) under
  Schlegel or "Prinz von Dänemark", and de.wikisource.org's search finds no transcription of it. A German
  player who recites Schlegel's line is therefore not refused for quoting: it shares "die Frage" with
  Wieland's but no four words running. "Seyn" is a Wiktionary function word (an old spelling of "sein",
  "his"), so the telling word of the line is "Frage".
- **Back-translation is one run.** An independent check re-ran the meaning step on a sample with the same
  script and models and got different back-translations for 38 of 175 items scored on the iMac and 11 of 25
  Korean items scored on the Studio (cosine moved by up to 0.53), though no item crossed the flag rule. The
  output depends on the host and on which items share a batch. `backtranslation.json` records one run; treat
  its scores as indicative, not as reproducible numbers. The meaning rule (both MiniLM and LaBSE under 0.6)
  catches only gross mismatches: it passed the Arabic RL-006 line that read "Anxiety lies on the head", fixed
  in this round to the circumstantial قلقًا on a reading of the grammar, not by a tool.
- The telling words of a line depend on `Intl.Segmenter` for Chinese, Japanese and Thai, so they can differ
  slightly between browsers and Node versions.
- **Names recorded as missing are shown in English**, in the card's name and in scenes (Arabic and Thai scenes
  name Oberon, Horatio and Malvolio in Latin letters). The Japanese scene for Mercutio writes マキューシオ although
  the card shows "Mercutio": the name has no Wikidata label and no ja.wikipedia article, and Tsubouchi's
  published Romeo and Juliet, which names him, was not read for names. Scenes are not shown on any page yet.
- **The card detail view** (`src/pages/collection.astro`) is being translated on another branch
  (lang/pages-core), which marks the card's name, line and scene `data-card-text` and leaves them to this
  work. This branch does not touch that page; when the two meet, those spots take `cardName`, `cardQuote`
  and `cardScene` from `src/lib/cards-i18n` with the interface language. Until then the battle page is the
  only page that shows card names in the interface language.

## How to rebuild

```
# names (Wikidata), texts (Wikisource) and lines (patterns): scripts/language/fetch/
python3 scripts/language/fetch/names.py            # writes wikidata-card-names.json
python3 scripts/language/fetch/names_ptbr.py       # writes wikidata-pt-br.json (Brazilian Portuguese labels of the same items)
python3 scripts/language/fetch/aozora.py ja rj 42773   # an Aozora Bunko text, pinned by the sha256 of its file
python3 -I scripts/language/fetch/closed_class.py <empty dir>   # Wiktionary function words (kaikki.org) -> wiktionary-closed-class.json
python3 scripts/language/fetch/fetchwork.py fr.wikisource.org fr hamlet "Le second Hamlet (trad. Hugo)"
python3 scripts/language/fetch/extract.py fr scripts/language/fetch/specs/fr.txt
# then, from the repository root (needs: pip install regex opencc-python-reimplemented)
```
